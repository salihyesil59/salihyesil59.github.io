21-cm line

Radio emission or absorption at 1420.4 MHz (21.1 cm) caused by the flip of the electron spin relative to the proton spin in hydrogen. Redshifted, it maps neutral hydrogen during the dark ages, cosmic dawn and reionisation.

See also: Reionisation, Redshift (z)

Explained in: L6.4 Reionisation and 21-cm Cosmology

Acoustic peaks

The series of maxima in the CMB power spectrum, produced by sound waves in the photon–baryon plasma caught at maximum compression or rarefaction when the CMB was released. The first lies at ℓ ≈ 220, about one degree.

See also: Angular power spectrum (Cℓ), Sound horizon, Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)

Explained in: L5.1 CMB Anisotropies: The Power Spectrum and Acoustic Peaks

Age of the universe

The time elapsed since the Big Bang, about 13.8 billion years according to the ΛCDM model. It follows from integrating the expansion history, and is close to the Hubble time 1/H0 by coincidence.

See also: Hubble time, ΛCDM model

Explained in: L3.4 The ΛCDM Model, L1.2 The Hubble–Lemaître Law, L3.5 Computing the Age of the Universe

Angular diameter distance

The distance obtained by comparing an object's true size with the angle it covers on the sky. In an expanding universe it differs from other distance measures, and it even decreases again for very distant objects.

See also: Luminosity distance, Comoving distance

Explained in: L2.1 The Scale Factor: Comoving and Proper Distance, L2.7 Distance Measures: Luminosity Distance, Angular Diameter Distance, Lookback Time

Angular power spectrum (Cℓ)

The strength of temperature fluctuations on the sky as a function of multipole ℓ, i.e. of angular scale. For the CMB it is usually plotted as ℓ(ℓ+1)Cℓ/2π in μK².

See also: Multipole (ℓ), CMB anisotropies, Acoustic peaks

Explained in: L5.1 CMB Anisotropies: The Power Spectrum and Acoustic Peaks

Annual modulation

The expected yearly swing of a few per cent in the dark matter rate, peaking in early June when the Earth's orbital motion adds most to the Sun's motion through the halo. DAMA/LIBRA reports one; other experiments do not.

See also: Direct detection

Explained in: L3.6 Hunting Dark Matter: WIMPs, Axions and the Experiments That Look for Them

Antimatter

Matter made of antiparticles, which have the same mass as their partners but opposite charge. A particle and its antiparticle annihilate into energy. The observable universe contains almost no antimatter.

See also: Baryogenesis, Baryon

Explained in: L4.6 Baryogenesis: The Matter–Antimatter Asymmetry

arXiv

The free online preprint server where physicists and astronomers post their papers, usually before (and sometimes instead of) journal publication. Almost every cosmology result appears there first.

See also: Look-elsewhere effect, Blinding

Explained in: L7.7 Reading a Cosmology Paper: From the Abstract to the Contour Plot

Astronomical unit (AU)

Exactly 149 597 870 700 metres, roughly the average Earth–Sun distance. Used for distances within the Solar System.

See also: Light-year (ly), Parsec (pc)

Explained in: L0.2 Distance Units: AU, Light-Year, Parsec, Megaparsec

Axion

A very light hypothetical particle, proposed to explain why the strong nuclear force respects the symmetry between matter and its mirror image. Axions of about a micro-electronvolt would make excellent cold dark matter.

See also: Dark matter, WIMP

Explained in: L3.6 Hunting Dark Matter: WIMPs, Axions and the Experiments That Look for Them, L6.8 Open Problems: Dark Matter, Dark Energy, Quantum Gravity and the Multiverse

B mode

The curl part of a polarisation pattern, swirling around a spot with a handedness. Scalar density ripples cannot make it, so a primordial B mode would mean gravitational waves from inflation. Lensing of the E modes makes a smaller one that has already been detected.

See also: E mode, CMB lensing, Tensor-to-scalar ratio r

Explained in: L5.7 CMB Polarisation and Lensing: E-modes, B-modes and What They Carry

Baryogenesis

The unknown process in the early universe that created a small excess of baryons over antibaryons, leaving the matter we see today.

See also: Sakharov conditions, Leptogenesis, Baryon-to-photon ratio (η)

Explained in: L4.6 Baryogenesis: The Matter–Antimatter Asymmetry, L6.8 Open Problems: Dark Matter, Dark Energy, Quantum Gravity and the Multiverse

Baryon

A particle made of three quarks, such as a proton or neutron. In cosmology, "baryonic matter" means all ordinary matter made of atoms (including electrons), about 5% of the universe's energy content.

See also: Dark matter, Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN)

Explained in: L3.2 Evidence for Dark Matter, L4.3 Big Bang Nucleosynthesis, L4.6 Baryogenesis: The Matter–Antimatter Asymmetry

Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)

A slight excess of galaxy pairs separated by the sound horizon at the drag epoch, about 147 Mpc, left by sound waves in the early universe. Used as a standard ruler to measure distances and the expansion rate.

See also: Sound horizon, Standard ruler, Two-point correlation function ξ(r)

Explained in: L5.2 Baryon Acoustic Oscillations

Baryon-to-photon ratio (η)

The number of baryons per photon, about 6 × 10⁻¹⁰. There are more than a billion CMB photons for every proton or neutron. This ratio controls nucleosynthesis and delays recombination.

See also: Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), Recombination

Explained in: L4.2 Temperature in an Expanding Universe, L4.3 Big Bang Nucleosynthesis, L4.6 Baryogenesis: The Matter–Antimatter Asymmetry

Big Bang

The hot, dense early state of the universe and its subsequent expansion and cooling. It was not an explosion at a point in space; space itself expanded everywhere.

See also: Scale factor a(t), Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), Cosmic microwave background (CMB)

Explained in: L4.1 A Timeline of the Universe, L1.3 The Cosmological Principle, L0.5 A History of Cosmology: From Geocentrism to the Big Bang

Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN)

The formation of light nuclei (deuterium, helium-3, helium-4, lithium-7) in the first twenty minutes after the Big Bang.

See also: Helium-4, Deuterium, Baryon-to-photon ratio (η)

Explained in: L4.3 Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

Big Crunch

A possible fate in which gravity halts the expansion and the universe collapses back into a hot, dense state. Observations of dark energy make it very unlikely for our universe.

See also: Closed universe, Friedmann equations

Explained in: L2.3 The Friedmann Equations

Big Rip

A possible end of the universe if dark energy is phantom (w < −1). Its density grows as space expands, the expansion rate becomes infinite in a finite time, and every bound system — clusters, galaxies, planets and finally atoms — is torn apart.

See also: Phantom energy, Big Crunch, Heat death

Explained in: L6.10 The Far Future: Heat Death, Big Rip or Big Crunch

Black hole shadow

The dark region an observer sees around a black hole, where light rays traced backwards end on the horizon instead of escaping. Its angular diameter is 2√27 GM/c²D, about 2.6 times the horizon radius, and depends only on mass and distance — which makes imaging it a test of general relativity.

See also: Schwarzschild radius, Event horizon (cosmological)

Explained in: L6.9 Black Holes in Cosmology: From the First Image to Primordial Black Holes

Blackbody radiation

The light emitted by an object in thermal equilibrium. Its spectrum depends only on temperature. The cosmic microwave background is the most perfect blackbody ever measured.

See also: Cosmic microwave background (CMB), Spectrum

Explained in: L4.2 Temperature in an Expanding Universe, L4.4 Recombination and the Last Scattering Surface, L1.5 The Discovery of the Cosmic Microwave Background

Blinding

Hiding the true result of an analysis, for instance by shifting the data by an unknown amount, until the method has been tested and frozen. It stops analysts from tuning their choices, consciously or not, towards an expected answer.

See also: Null test, Systematic error

Explained in: L7.6 Systematics: The Errors That Do Not Shrink with More Data

Blueshift

A shift of light to shorter wavelengths, caused for example by a source moving towards the observer. The opposite of redshift.

See also: Redshift (z), Doppler effect

Explained in: L0.3 Light, Spectra, the Doppler Effect and Redshift

Bullet Cluster

Two colliding galaxy clusters (1E 0657-558) in which gravitational lensing shows that most of the mass has separated from the hot gas that contains most of the ordinary matter. Strong evidence for dark matter.

See also: Dark matter, Gravitational lensing

Explained in: L3.2 Evidence for Dark Matter

Cepheid variable

A pulsating giant star whose pulsation period is tightly related to its luminosity (the Leavitt law). This makes Cepheids standard candles for measuring distances to nearby galaxies.

See also: Standard candle, Cosmic distance ladder, Leavitt law (period–luminosity relation)

Explained in: L1.1 The Cosmic Distance Ladder, L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence

Chi-squared (χ²)

The sum of squared differences between data and model, each divided by its own uncertainty. Near one per degree of freedom means a plausible fit; how fast it rises around the minimum gives the error bars.

See also: Likelihood, Degrees of freedom

Explained in: L7.2 From Data to a Number: Likelihood, χ² and 5σ, L7.1 Reading a Scientific Plot

Chirp mass

The combination (m₁m₂)^(3/5)/(m₁+m₂)^(1/5) of the masses of a binary that controls how fast the frequency of its gravitational waves increases.

See also: Gravitational waves, Standard siren

Explained in: L6.5 Gravitational Waves and Standard Sirens

Closed universe

A universe with positive spatial curvature (k > 0, total density above critical). It is finite but has no edge, like the surface of a sphere in two dimensions.

See also: Spatial curvature, Open universe, Flat universe

Explained in: L2.5 The Geometry of Space: Open, Flat, Closed, L2.4 Critical Density and Density Parameters

CMB anisotropies

The tiny temperature differences, about 100 μK or one part in 30 000, between different directions in the cosmic microwave background. They record density variations 380 000 years after the Big Bang.

See also: Cosmic microwave background (CMB), Angular power spectrum (Cℓ)

Explained in: L5.1 CMB Anisotropies: The Power Spectrum and Acoustic Peaks

CMB lensing

The deflection of microwave-background photons, by a few arcminutes each, by all the matter between the last-scattering surface and us. It smooths the acoustic peaks, converts some E modes into B modes, and measures how much structure has grown.

See also: Weak lensing, B mode, σ8

Explained in: L5.7 CMB Polarisation and Lensing: E-modes, B-modes and What They Carry, L5.6 Gravitational Lensing: Strong and Weak

CMB polarisation

The few-percent linear polarisation of the microwave background, produced by Thomson scattering off electrons that see a quadrupole in the radiation around them. Because that quadrupole comes from the plasma's velocity, the polarisation peaks fall where the temperature peaks do not.

See also: E mode, B mode, Cosmic microwave background (CMB)

Explained in: L5.7 CMB Polarisation and Lensing: E-modes, B-modes and What They Carry

Comoving distance

Distance measured on a grid that expands with the universe. It stays constant for objects moving with the expansion. By convention it equals today's proper distance.

See also: Proper distance, Scale factor a(t)

Explained in: L2.1 The Scale Factor: Comoving and Proper Distance, L2.7 Distance Measures: Luminosity Distance, Angular Diameter Distance, Lookback Time

Confidence contour

The region of a two-parameter plane that holds a given fraction (usually 68% or 95%) of the probability. Its tilt shows which combination of the two parameters the data actually measure.

See also: Posterior, Systematic error

Explained in: L7.1 Reading a Scientific Plot

Convergence (R-hat)

Whether several MCMC chains started far apart have settled into describing the same distribution. R̂ compares the spread within each chain with the spread between them; values above about 1.01 mean the chains are not finished.

See also: MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo)

Explained in: L7.2 From Data to a Number: Likelihood, χ² and 5σ

Cosmic dawn

The era when the first stars and galaxies lit up, ending the dark ages, roughly 100–300 million years after the Big Bang (z ≈ 30–10). It is followed by reionisation.

See also: Dark ages, Population III stars, Reionisation

Explained in: L4.8 The Dark Ages and Cosmic Dawn: The First Stars and Galaxies, L6.4 Reionisation and 21-cm Cosmology

Cosmic distance ladder

The chain of methods (radar, parallax, Cepheids, Type Ia supernovae, redshift) in which each method is calibrated by the one below it to measure ever larger distances.

See also: Parallax, Cepheid variable, Type Ia supernova, Leavitt law (period–luminosity relation)

Explained in: L1.1 The Cosmic Distance Ladder, L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence

Cosmic inflation

A proposed phase of accelerated expansion before the hot Big Bang, driven by a slowly rolling scalar field. It explains the flatness and uniformity of the universe and the origin of the primordial fluctuations.

See also: Inflaton, e-fold, Horizon problem

Explained in: L6.3 Inflation: Horizon, Flatness and Monopole Problems, Slow Roll, nₛ and r

Cosmic microwave background (CMB)

The relic radiation released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when atoms formed. Today it fills the universe as microwaves with a blackbody temperature of 2.7255 K and tiny fluctuations of one part in 100 000.

See also: Recombination, Last scattering surface, Blackbody radiation

Explained in: L4.4 Recombination and the Last Scattering Surface, L1.3 The Cosmological Principle, L1.5 The Discovery of the Cosmic Microwave Background, L5.1 CMB Anisotropies: The Power Spectrum and Acoustic Peaks

Cosmic neutrino background (CνB)

The relic neutrinos that decoupled about one second after the Big Bang. They fill space with about 336 neutrinos per cubic centimetre at a temperature of 1.95 K, but have not been detected directly.

See also: Neutrino, Cosmic microwave background (CMB), Effective number of neutrino species (N_eff), Free streaming

Explained in: L4.5 Neutrino Decoupling and the Cosmic Neutrino Background, L4.7 Neutrino Mass: Weighing the Lightest Particles with the Universe

Cosmic void

A huge, nearly empty region of space, typically 10–100 Mpc across, surrounded by the filaments and walls of the cosmic web. Voids fill most of the universe's volume.

See also: Cosmic web

Explained in: L1.4 Galaxies, Clusters and the Cosmic Web

Cosmic web

The large-scale pattern in which galaxies are arranged: filaments and sheets of galaxies with clusters at their intersections, surrounding vast empty voids.

See also: Cosmic void, Galaxy cluster, Supercluster

Explained in: L1.4 Galaxies, Clusters and the Cosmic Web

Cosmological constant (Λ)

A constant term in Einstein's equations that acts like an energy of empty space with negative pressure (w = −1). It is the simplest explanation for dark energy.

See also: Dark energy, Equation of state (w), ΛCDM model

Explained in: L3.3 Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe, L3.1 Radiation, Matter, Dark Energy and the Equation of State, L6.2 Deriving the Friedmann Equations from Einstein's Field Equations

Cosmological principle

The assumption, supported by observations, that on large enough scales the universe is homogeneous (the same everywhere) and isotropic (the same in all directions).

See also: Homogeneity, Isotropy

Explained in: L1.3 The Cosmological Principle

Cosmological redshift

The stretching of light's wavelength by the expansion of space while the light travels. It obeys 1 + z = 1/a, where a is the scale factor when the light was emitted.

See also: Redshift (z), Scale factor a(t)

Explained in: L2.2 Cosmological Redshift vs. Doppler Redshift

Cosmological tension

A statistically significant disagreement between measurements of the same quantity by different methods, such as the Hubble tension and the S8 tension. It may signal systematic errors or new physics.

See also: Hubble tension, σ8

Explained in: L6.6 Current Tensions: the Hubble Tension and the S8 Tension

Cosmology

The science of the universe as a whole: its contents, structure, origin, evolution and fate.

See also: Cosmological principle, Big Bang

Explained in: L0.1 Scales of the Universe

Covariance matrix

The table of how the uncertainties in a set of measurements move together. Its diagonal holds the squared error bars; the off-diagonal entries say how strongly a fluctuation in one bin drags another with it. For a real survey it is usually measured from thousands of mock catalogues.

See also: Mock catalogue, Statistical error, Sample variance (cosmic variance)

Explained in: L7.5 Simulations as Experiments: From Initial Conditions to a Mock Catalogue, L7.2 From Data to a Number: Likelihood, χ² and 5σ

CP violation

A difference between the behaviour of particles and the mirror image of their antiparticles. It is observed in quark and possibly neutrino interactions and is required to create the matter–antimatter asymmetry.

See also: Sakharov conditions, Baryogenesis

Explained in: L4.6 Baryogenesis: The Matter–Antimatter Asymmetry

Critical density

The density needed for a flat universe at a given expansion rate, 3H²/(8πG). Today it is about 9 × 10⁻²⁷ kg/m³, roughly five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre.

See also: Density parameter (Ω), Flat universe

Explained in: L2.4 Critical Density and Density Parameters

Dark ages

The period between the release of the CMB, 380 000 years after the Big Bang, and the first stars, some 100–200 million years later. The universe held only neutral gas, dark matter and the fading background light.

See also: Cosmic dawn, Recombination, 21-cm line

Explained in: L4.8 The Dark Ages and Cosmic Dawn: The First Stars and Galaxies, L6.4 Reionisation and 21-cm Cosmology

Dark energy

The unknown component with strongly negative pressure that makes the expansion of the universe accelerate. It accounts for about 69% of the energy density today.

See also: Cosmological constant (Λ), Deceleration parameter (q)

Explained in: L3.3 Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe, L3.4 The ΛCDM Model, L2.6 Horizons: Particle Horizon, Hubble Sphere, Event Horizon, L6.7 Alternative Models: MOND, Modified Gravity and Quintessence, L6.8 Open Problems: Dark Matter, Dark Energy, Quantum Gravity and the Multiverse

Dark matter

Matter that neither emits nor absorbs light but exerts gravity. It makes up about 84% of all matter and is revealed by rotation curves, cluster dynamics, gravitational lensing and the CMB.

See also: Rotation curve, Bullet Cluster, ΛCDM model

Explained in: L3.2 Evidence for Dark Matter, L5.3 Jeans Instability and Linear Perturbation Growth, L5.5 Hierarchical Structure Formation and N-body Simulations, L6.8 Open Problems: Dark Matter, Dark Energy, Quantum Gravity and the Multiverse

Dark matter halo

A gravitationally bound, roughly spherical concentration of dark matter, about 200 times denser than the cosmic average, that hosts a galaxy, group or cluster.

See also: Hierarchical structure formation, Halo mass function, Dark matter

Explained in: L5.3 Jeans Instability and Linear Perturbation Growth, L5.5 Hierarchical Structure Formation and N-body Simulations

Deceleration parameter (q)

A dimensionless measure of how the expansion rate changes, q = −ä·a/ȧ². Positive q means deceleration, negative q acceleration. Today q0 ≈ −0.53.

See also: Dark energy, Friedmann equations

Explained in: L3.3 Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe

Decoupling

The moment a type of particle stops interacting with the rest of the cosmic plasma because its reactions become slower than the expansion. Photons decoupled at recombination; neutrinos about one second after the Big Bang.

See also: Recombination, Neutrino

Explained in: L4.4 Recombination and the Last Scattering Surface, L4.2 Temperature in an Expanding Universe, L4.5 Neutrino Decoupling and the Cosmic Neutrino Background

Degrees of freedom

The number of data points minus the number of fitted parameters. Each one is expected to contribute about 1 to χ², so χ² divided by the degrees of freedom should be near 1.

See also: Chi-squared (χ²)

Explained in: L7.2 From Data to a Number: Likelihood, χ² and 5σ

Density contrast (δ)

The relative deviation of the local density from the cosmic average, δ = (ρ − ρ̄)/ρ̄. It was about 10⁻⁵ at recombination and exceeds a million in galaxies today.

See also: Linear growth factor D(a), Jeans length

Explained in: L5.3 Jeans Instability and Linear Perturbation Growth

Density parameter (Ω)

The density of a component divided by the critical density. Total Ω above 1 means closed space, equal to 1 flat, below 1 open.

See also: Critical density, Spatial curvature

Explained in: L2.4 Critical Density and Density Parameters

Deuterium

Heavy hydrogen: a nucleus of one proton and one neutron. Its primordial abundance is a sensitive measure of the density of ordinary matter.

See also: Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), Baryon

Explained in: L4.3 Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

Dimensional analysis

Checking or reconstructing a formula from the units of the quantities in it. It can prove an equation wrong in seconds and often gives the shape of the right answer, but never the dimensionless factor in front.

See also: Order-of-magnitude estimate

Explained in: L0.7 Orders of Magnitude: Estimating in Your Head Before You Compute

Direct detection

Searching for dark matter particles from the Galaxy's halo bouncing off atomic nuclei in a shielded detector deep underground. The nuclei recoil with a few keV.

See also: WIMP, Exclusion curve, Annual modulation

Explained in: L3.6 Hunting Dark Matter: WIMPs, Axions and the Experiments That Look for Them

Doppler effect

The change in observed wavelength of a wave caused by the relative motion of source and observer: longer when they recede, shorter when they approach.

See also: Redshift (z), Blueshift

Explained in: L0.3 Light, Spectra, the Doppler Effect and Redshift

E mode

The curl-free part of a polarisation pattern on the sky, whose directions point radially towards or tangentially around a spot. Density perturbations at the last-scattering surface produce E modes and nothing else, so the E-mode power spectrum of the CMB is a direct check on the acoustic picture.

See also: B mode, CMB polarisation, Optical depth (τ)

Explained in: L5.7 CMB Polarisation and Lensing: E-modes, B-modes and What They Carry

e-fold

Growth by a factor e ≈ 2.718. The expansion during inflation is counted in e-folds, N = ln(a_end/a_start); about 60 are needed to solve the horizon and flatness problems.

See also: Cosmic inflation

Explained in: L6.3 Inflation: Horizon, Flatness and Monopole Problems, Slow Roll, nₛ and r

Effective number of neutrino species (N_eff)

A parameter measuring the energy density of relativistic particles other than photons. The Standard Model predicts 3.044; nucleosynthesis and the CMB measure a value close to 3.

See also: Cosmic neutrino background (CνB), Radiation (in cosmology)

Explained in: L4.5 Neutrino Decoupling and the Cosmic Neutrino Background

Effective sample size

The number of statistically independent samples in a correlated chain: the chain length divided by the autocorrelation length. A long but sticky chain may contain only a few dozen independent samples.

See also: MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo), Convergence (R-hat)

Explained in: L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence

Effective volume

The volume a galaxy survey is worth once shot noise is taken into account: V_eff = V [n̄P / (1 + n̄P)]². A survey with too few galaxies behaves as if it had mapped only a small part of its real volume. BAO distance errors scale as 1/√V_eff.

See also: Shot noise, Sample variance (cosmic variance), Redshift survey

Explained in: L7.4 How a Survey Is Built: DESI, Euclid, Rubin and JWST

Einstein radius

The angular radius of the ring formed when a source lies exactly behind a lens. It depends only on the lens mass and on the distances, so it weighs the lens: about 1″ for galaxies and tens of arcseconds for clusters.

See also: Gravitational lensing, Weak lensing

Explained in: L5.6 Gravitational Lensing: Strong and Weak

Electromagnetic spectrum

The full range of light wavelengths, from gamma rays and X-rays through ultraviolet, visible and infrared light to microwaves and radio waves.

See also: Wavelength (λ), Spectrum

Explained in: L0.3 Light, Spectra, the Doppler Effect and Redshift

Electron volt (eV)

A unit of energy, 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules. A temperature of 11 600 K corresponds to a typical particle energy of 1 eV; 1 MeV corresponds to about 10¹⁰ K.

See also: Blackbody radiation

Explained in: L4.2 Temperature in an Expanding Universe

Equation of state (w)

The ratio of pressure to energy density, w = p/(ρc²). Matter has w = 0, radiation w = 1/3 and a cosmological constant w = −1. It determines how a component dilutes as the universe expands.

See also: Matter (in cosmology), Radiation (in cosmology), Cosmological constant (Λ)

Explained in: L3.1 Radiation, Matter, Dark Energy and the Equation of State

Etherington relation

The distance duality relation D_L = (1 + z)² D_A between luminosity distance and angular diameter distance, valid in any expanding universe in which photons are conserved.

See also: Luminosity distance, Angular diameter distance

Explained in: L2.7 Distance Measures: Luminosity Distance, Angular Diameter Distance, Lookback Time

Event horizon (cosmological)

The greatest distance from which light emitted now can ever reach us. It exists only if the expansion accelerates forever; for our universe it lies about 16.6 billion light-years away today.

See also: Particle horizon, Hubble sphere, Dark energy

Explained in: L2.6 Horizons: Particle Horizon, Hubble Sphere, Event Horizon

Exclusion curve

The line in the plane of dark matter mass and cross-section above which an experiment that saw nothing rules a particle out, because it would have produced too many events. Below the curve a particle could still hide.

See also: Direct detection, Neutrino fog

Explained in: L3.6 Hunting Dark Matter: WIMPs, Axions and the Experiments That Look for Them

Feedback (galaxy formation)

Energy returned to the gas by the products of star formation: supernovae and stellar winds in small galaxies, jets and winds from accreting black holes in massive ones. It keeps galaxy formation inefficient.

See also: Stellar-to-halo mass relation, Quenching

Explained in: L5.8 Galaxy Formation: Cooling, Feedback and How Galaxies Live in Their Haloes

Flat universe

A universe with zero spatial curvature, where ordinary Euclidean geometry holds on large scales. Its total density equals the critical density. Observations show our universe is flat to within about 0.2%.

See also: Spatial curvature, Critical density

Explained in: L2.5 The Geometry of Space: Open, Flat, Closed

FLRW metric

The Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric, the most general description of a homogeneous and isotropic expanding spacetime. It depends only on the scale factor a(t) and the spatial curvature k.

See also: Metric, Scale factor a(t), Spatial curvature

Explained in: L6.1 Minimal General Relativity: Metrics, Geodesics and the FLRW Metric, L6.2 Deriving the Friedmann Equations from Einstein's Field Equations

Free streaming

Fast-moving particles such as relic neutrinos travel out of regions smaller than the distance they cover, smoothing out density fluctuations on those scales. It is why massive neutrinos suppress the small-scale matter power spectrum, and why hot dark matter cannot form galaxies.

See also: Neutrino, Matter power spectrum P(k), Dark matter

Explained in: L4.7 Neutrino Mass: Weighing the Lightest Particles with the Universe

Friedmann equations

The equations, derived from general relativity for a homogeneous and isotropic universe, that relate the expansion rate and acceleration of the scale factor to the density, pressure and curvature of the universe.

See also: Scale factor a(t), Critical density

Explained in: L2.3 The Friedmann Equations

Galaxy

A gravitationally bound system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter, containing from millions to hundreds of billions of stars.

See also: Milky Way, Local Group

Explained in: L0.1 Scales of the Universe, L1.4 Galaxies, Clusters and the Cosmic Web

Galaxy bias

How much more strongly a population of galaxies clusters than the underlying matter. Massive red galaxies, living in the densest regions, have a high bias; star-forming galaxies a lower one. A higher bias means more signal per galaxy.

See also: Redshift survey, Matter power spectrum P(k)

Explained in: L7.4 How a Survey Is Built: DESI, Euclid, Rubin and JWST

Galaxy cluster

A gravitationally bound system of hundreds to thousands of galaxies with a total mass of 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ solar masses, filled with X-ray emitting hot gas and dominated by dark matter. The largest bound objects in the universe.

See also: Local Group, Supercluster, Dark matter

Explained in: L1.4 Galaxies, Clusters and the Cosmic Web, L3.2 Evidence for Dark Matter

General relativity

Einstein's theory of gravity (1915), in which mass and energy curve spacetime and free objects follow the curvature. It is the basis of modern cosmology.

See also: Spacetime, Friedmann equations

Explained in: L0.4 Gravity, Intuitively: From Newton to Einstein, L6.1 Minimal General Relativity: Metrics, Geodesics and the FLRW Metric, L6.2 Deriving the Friedmann Equations from Einstein's Field Equations

Geocentric model

The ancient picture of the cosmos with a stationary Earth at the centre and the Sun, Moon, planets and stars moving around it. Ptolemy's version with epicycles was used for about 1 400 years.

See also: Heliocentric model

Explained in: L0.5 A History of Cosmology: From Geocentrism to the Big Bang

Geodesic

The straightest possible path in a curved space or spacetime. In general relativity freely falling particles and light rays follow geodesics.

See also: Metric, General relativity

Explained in: L6.1 Minimal General Relativity: Metrics, Geodesics and the FLRW Metric

Globular cluster

A dense, spherical group of hundreds of thousands of old stars orbiting in a galaxy's halo. Their stellar populations give ages of about 12–13.5 billion years, a lower limit on the age of the universe.

See also: Age of the universe, Milky Way

Explained in: L3.5 Computing the Age of the Universe

Gravitational lensing

The bending of light by mass, predicted by general relativity. It distorts, magnifies or multiplies the images of background sources and measures the total mass of the lens, including dark matter.

In a sample limited by apparent brightness, distant objects are detected only if they are intrinsically bright, so the catalogue is brighter on average than the population. Uncorrected, it makes distant standard candles look closer than they are.

Matter (in cosmology)

Any component whose particles move much slower than light, so its pressure is negligible (w = 0). Includes ordinary (baryonic) matter and cold dark matter. Its density dilutes as a⁻³.

Neutrino

A very light, electrically neutral particle that interacts only weakly. Neutrinos decoupled about one second after the Big Bang and still fill the universe as a cosmic neutrino background.

Neutrino fog

The background of solar, atmospheric and supernova neutrinos scattering coherently off nuclei. It looks like a dark matter signal and slows the progress of direct detection below cross-sections of about 10⁻⁴⁸–10⁻⁴⁹ cm².

Neutrino mass ordering

Neutrino oscillation

The change of a neutrino from one flavour to another as it travels. It happens only because the flavours are mixtures of states with different masses, so its discovery in 1998 proved that neutrinos have mass. Oscillations measure differences of squared masses, not the masses themselves.

Observable universe

The region from which light has had time to reach us since the Big Bang. Because space expanded while the light travelled, its radius today is about 46 billion light-years. The whole universe may be much larger or infinite.

The puzzle that the night sky is dark although an infinite, eternal, static universe full of stars would make it bright everywhere. It is resolved mainly by the finite age of the universe and the finite lifetimes of stars.

Open universe

A universe with negative spatial curvature (k < 0, total density below critical), like a saddle surface in two dimensions. It is infinite.

Primordial black hole

A black hole formed in the very early universe from an unusually large density fluctuation rather than from a star. Radiation (in cosmology)

The formation of neutral atoms about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe cooled to about 3 000 K. It made the universe transparent and released the CMB.

The balance between ionisation and recombination in thermal equilibrium. Applied to hydrogen in the early universe it predicts recombination at about 3 700 K; the real universe falls out of equilibrium and recombines a little later.

Sample variance (cosmic variance)

The uncertainty that comes from observing only a finite piece of the universe, whose structures are one random realisation. It can be reduced only by covering more volume; on the largest scales even the whole observable universe is a single sample.

Scale factor a(t)

The factor by which all cosmic distances are multiplied as the universe expands. By convention a = 1 today and a = 1/(1 + z) when light of redshift z was emitted.

Schwarzschild radius

The radius of the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole, r_s = 2GM/c². It grows in proportion to the mass, so the mean density inside falls as 1/M²: a supermassive black hole is less dense than water.

The probability that an object which exists is actually in the catalogue, as a function of distance, brightness, colour and position on the sky. Sound horizon

The comoving distance a sound wave in the photon–baryon plasma could travel before recombination: about 144 Mpc at decoupling and 147 Mpc at the drag epoch. It sets the scale of the acoustic peaks and BAO.

See also: Acoustic peaks, Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), Standard ruler

Explained in: L5.1 CMB Anisotropies: The Power Spectrum and Acoustic Peaks, L5.2 Baryon Acoustic Oscillations

Spacetime

The four-dimensional combination of three space dimensions and time used in relativity. In general relativity mass and energy curve spacetime.

See also: General relativity

Explained in: L0.4 Gravity, Intuitively: From Newton to Einstein, L6.1 Minimal General Relativity: Metrics, Geodesics and the FLRW Metric

Spatial curvature

The deviation of space from flat (Euclidean) geometry. It can be positive (closed), zero (flat) or negative (open), and is measured by k or by the curvature parameter Ωk.

See also: Flat universe, Closed universe, Open universe

Explained in: L2.5 The Geometry of Space: Open, Flat, Closed, L2.3 The Friedmann Equations

Spectral line

A narrow dark (absorption) or bright (emission) feature in a spectrum at a wavelength characteristic of a particular element or molecule.

See also: Spectrum, Redshift (z)

Explained in: L0.3 Light, Spectra, the Doppler Effect and Redshift

Spectrum

Light spread out by wavelength. It reveals an object's temperature, composition and motion.

See also: Spectral line, Electromagnetic spectrum

Explained in: L0.3 Light, Spectra, the Doppler Effect and Redshift

Sphaleron

A process in the Standard Model, efficient at temperatures above about 100 GeV, that changes the numbers of baryons and leptons while conserving their difference.

See also: Leptogenesis, Baryogenesis

Explained in: L4.6 Baryogenesis: The Matter–Antimatter Asymmetry

Spin temperature

The excitation temperature of hydrogen's 21-cm hyperfine transition. The 21-cm line is seen in absorption when it is below the radio background and in emission when it is above; it is set by collisions, Lyman-α photons and the CMB.

See also: 21-cm line, Cosmic dawn

Explained in: L6.4 Reionisation and 21-cm Cosmology, L4.8 The Dark Ages and Cosmic Dawn: The First Stars and Galaxies

Standard candle

An astronomical object of known luminosity. Comparing its known luminosity with its observed brightness gives its distance.

See also: Cepheid variable, Type Ia supernova, Luminosity

Explained in: L1.1 The Cosmic Distance Ladder, L6.5 Gravitational Waves and Standard Sirens

Standard ruler

An object or feature of known physical size. Its apparent angular size gives the angular diameter distance. The BAO scale and the CMB sound horizon are the most important cosmological rulers.

See also: Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), Angular diameter distance, Standard candle

Explained in: L5.2 Baryon Acoustic Oscillations

Standard siren

A source of gravitational waves, such as merging neutron stars, whose signal reveals its luminosity distance directly from general relativity, without a distance ladder.

See also: Gravitational waves, Standard candle, Hubble constant (H0)

Explained in: L6.5 Gravitational Waves and Standard Sirens

Statistical error

The random part of a measurement's uncertainty, which shrinks as the square root of the number of measurements. The error bars on a plot usually show one standard deviation of it.

See also: Systematic error, Chi-squared (χ²)

Explained in: L7.1 Reading a Scientific Plot

Steady state theory

A cosmological model (Bondi, Gold and Hoyle, 1948) in which the universe expands but always looks the same, with matter continuously created. It was abandoned after the discovery of the cosmic microwave background.

See also: Big Bang, Cosmic microwave background (CMB)

Explained in: L0.5 A History of Cosmology: From Geocentrism to the Big Bang, L1.5 The Discovery of the Cosmic Microwave Background

Stellar-to-halo mass relation

How much stellar mass a galaxy has for a given dark matter halo mass. Found by matching the abundances of galaxies and haloes, it peaks at a few per cent near 10¹² M☉ and falls steeply for both smaller and larger haloes.

See also: Dark matter halo, Halo mass function, Feedback (galaxy formation)

Explained in: L5.8 Galaxy Formation: Cooling, Feedback and How Galaxies Live in Their Haloes

Supercluster

A large grouping of galaxy clusters and groups, part of the cosmic web. The Milky Way lies in the Laniakea supercluster, about 520 million light-years across.

See also: Galaxy, Local Group

Explained in: L0.1 Scales of the Universe, L1.4 Galaxies, Clusters and the Cosmic Web

Supermassive black hole

A black hole with millions to billions of solar masses, found at the centre of almost every large galaxy. The Milky Way's, Sagittarius A*, has about four million solar masses.

See also: Quasar, Milky Way

Explained in: L1.4 Galaxies, Clusters and the Cosmic Web

Systematic error

A bias that repeats in every measurement — a calibration offset, unmodelled dust, a selection effect. Unlike statistical noise it does not shrink as more data are collected, and it is usually what cosmological disputes are about.

See also: Statistical error, Hubble tension, Null test

Explained in: L7.1 Reading a Scientific Plot, L6.6 Current Tensions: the Hubble Tension and the S8 Tension, L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence, L7.6 Systematics: The Errors That Do Not Shrink with More Data

Tensor-to-scalar ratio r

The power in primordial gravitational waves divided by the power in density perturbations. It fixes the energy scale of inflation, V^(1/4) ≈ 10^16 GeV (r/0.01)^(1/4), and would be measured from B modes at ℓ ≈ 80. The limit today is r < 0.036.

See also: B mode, Cosmic inflation, Gravitational waves

Explained in: L5.7 CMB Polarisation and Lensing: E-modes, B-modes and What They Carry, L6.3 Inflation: Horizon, Flatness and Monopole Problems, Slow Roll, nₛ and r

Transfer function T(k)

Describes how the growth of each Fourier mode was modified after it entered the horizon. Modes that entered during radiation domination grew less, so T(k) falls on small scales.

See also: Matter power spectrum P(k)

Explained in: L5.4 The Matter Power Spectrum

Transverse comoving distance

The comoving distance corrected for the curvature of space, used to relate comoving sizes and angles. It equals the comoving distance in a flat universe.

See also: Comoving distance, Spatial curvature

Explained in: L2.7 Distance Measures: Luminosity Distance, Angular Diameter Distance, Lookback Time

Triangle plot

The standard figure of a parameter analysis: the one-dimensional posterior of each parameter on the diagonal and 68% / 95% contours for every pair below it, all computed from the same MCMC chain. Also called a corner plot.

See also: Confidence contour, Parameter degeneracy, MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo), Marginalisation

Explained in: L7.7 Reading a Cosmology Paper: From the Abstract to the Contour Plot, L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence

Two-point correlation function ξ(r)

The excess probability, compared with a random distribution, of finding two galaxies separated by a distance r. It is the Fourier counterpart of the power spectrum.

See also: Matter power spectrum P(k), Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)

Explained in: L5.2 Baryon Acoustic Oscillations

Type Ia supernova

The thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf star. Its peak luminosity can be standardised, making it a precise distance indicator out to billions of light-years. Used to discover cosmic acceleration.

See also: Standard candle, Dark energy, Cosmic distance ladder

Explained in: L1.1 The Cosmic Distance Ladder, L3.3 Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe

Virial temperature

The temperature gas reaches when it falls into a halo and its thermal energy balances gravity. Gas has to radiate it away to form stars: haloes above about 10⁸ M☉, at 10⁴ K, can cool by atomic hydrogen.

See also: Dark matter halo, Population III stars

Explained in: L4.8 The Dark Ages and Cosmic Dawn: The First Stars and Galaxies

Visibility function

The probability that a CMB photon last scattered at a given redshift. It peaks at z ≈ 1090 and has a width of about 200 in redshift: the last-scattering surface is a shell, not a sharp surface.

See also: Last scattering surface, Recombination, Saha equation

Explained in: L4.4 Recombination and the Last Scattering Surface

Wavelength (λ)

The distance between successive crests of a wave. For light it determines the colour or the band of the electromagnetic spectrum.

See also: Electromagnetic spectrum, Redshift (z)

Explained in: L0.3 Light, Spectra, the Doppler Effect and Redshift

Weak lensing

Tiny, percent-level distortions of the shapes of distant galaxies caused by intervening mass. Measured statistically, it maps dark matter and the growth of structure (cosmic shear).

See also: Gravitational lensing, Einstein radius, σ8

Explained in: L5.6 Gravitational Lensing: Strong and Weak

WIMP

Weakly interacting massive particle: a hypothetical dark matter particle of roughly 10–1000 GeV. Freezing out of the hot early universe, it would naturally leave about the observed amount of dark matter. None has been found.

See also: Dark matter, Direct detection, Axion

Explained in: L3.6 Hunting Dark Matter: WIMPs, Axions and the Experiments That Look for Them, L6.8 Open Problems: Dark Matter, Dark Energy, Quantum Gravity and the Multiverse

Zel'dovich approximation

The first-order solution for gravitational collapse: instead of integrating the forces, each particle is simply displaced from its starting point along the gradient of the initial potential. It is exact while fluctuations are small and is used to set the initial conditions of almost every simulation.

See also: N-body simulation, Mock catalogue, Hierarchical structure formation

ΛCDM model

The standard model of cosmology: a flat universe containing a cosmological constant (Λ), cold dark matter (CDM), ordinary matter and radiation, described by six parameters.

See also: Cosmological constant (Λ), Dark matter

Explained in: L3.4 The ΛCDM Model

σ8

The root-mean-square linear matter fluctuation in spheres of radius 8 Mpc/h today, the standard measure of the amplitude of clustering. Planck finds about 0.81.

See also: Matter power spectrum P(k), Weak lensing

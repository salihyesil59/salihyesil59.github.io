L1.3 The Cosmological Principle

On the largest scales the universe looks the same everywhere and in every direction. This simple assumption makes cosmology possible.

≈ 12 min read · Before this: L1.2 · Simulators: Balloon & Raisin-Bread Expansion

In this lesson you will learn

  • The meaning of homogeneity and isotropy
  • The observational evidence for the cosmological principle
  • Why uniform expansion has no centre
  • Why the principle hugely simplifies the mathematics

A modest assumption

For centuries people placed the Earth, then the Sun, at the centre of the universe. Each time, observations showed that our location is not special. The cosmological principle takes this lesson to its logical end:

The cosmological principle

On sufficiently large scales, the universe is homogeneous and isotropic.

  • Homogeneous means the same at every place: an average cube of 1 Gpc looks like any other such cube, wherever you put it.
  • Isotropic means the same in every direction: whichever way you look, you see the same average properties.

"On sufficiently large scales" matters. On small scales the universe is very lumpy: planets, stars, galaxies and voids. The principle is about averages over regions hundreds of megaparsecs across, in the same way that a sandy beach looks smooth from a helicopter even though it is made of individual grains.

The evidence

  1. The cosmic microwave background. The oldest light in the universe arrives from all directions with the same temperature, 2.7255 K, to about one part in 100 000 (lesson L4.4). This is extraordinary isotropy.
  2. Galaxy surveys. Maps of millions of galaxies, such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, show a cosmic web of filaments and voids. Above a few hundred megaparsecs, the web looks statistically the same everywhere.
  3. Distant radio sources and X-ray background are spread evenly over the sky.

Isotropy everywhere implies homogeneity

If the universe looks isotropic from our location, and we are not in a special place (the Copernican principle), then it looks isotropic from every location. A universe isotropic about every point must be homogeneous.

Expansion without a centre

The Hubble–Lemaître law is the only way a homogeneous and isotropic universe can expand. Picture galaxies on a stretching grid. Seen from galaxy A, galaxy B at distance moves away at . Now move to galaxy B: the grid stretches the same way, so B sees A (and everything else) receding according to the same law.

The Big Bang was not an explosion in space

It is tempting to picture the Big Bang as a bomb exploding at a point and galaxies flying outward into empty space. That picture has a centre and an edge, and it is wrong. Instead, space itself expands everywhere at once, carrying galaxies with it. There is no centre and no outside.

Why the principle is so powerful

Without the cosmological principle, describing the universe would require knowing the density, pressure and motion at every point: an impossible task. With it, the entire universe at any moment is described by just a few numbers, such as its average density and its expansion rate. Mathematically, the geometry of the whole universe reduces to one function of time, the scale factor, which you will meet in the next level.

Remember this

  • The cosmological principle: on large scales the universe is homogeneous (same everywhere) and isotropic (same in all directions).
  • It is supported by the CMB and by galaxy surveys.
  • Uniform expansion obeys for every observer; there is no centre.
  • The principle reduces cosmology to a few numbers that change with time.

Quiz

Choose an answer to see whether it is right, and why. Score 70% or more and you know this lesson.

1. What does 'isotropic' mean?

Isotropy means no preferred direction. Homogeneity means no preferred location.

2. On what scales does the cosmological principle apply?

On small scales the universe is lumpy. Averaged over regions hundreds of Mpc across, it looks the same everywhere.

3. Which observation provides the strongest evidence for isotropy?

The CMB has the same temperature in all directions to extraordinary precision.

4. Which picture of the Big Bang is correct?

The Big Bang happened everywhere: space expands and carries galaxies with it.

5. Why is the cosmological principle so useful?

With homogeneity and isotropy, quantities like density and expansion rate are the same everywhere, so only their time dependence matters.

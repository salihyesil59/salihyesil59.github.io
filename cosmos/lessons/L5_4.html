L5.4 The Matter Power Spectrum

How strongly is matter clumped on each scale? The power spectrum answers this question, and its shape records the whole history of the early universe.

Clumpiness, scale by scale

The universe is lumpy on some scales and smooth on others. To describe this, cosmologists break the density field into waves of different wavelengths, exactly like splitting sound into frequencies. Each wave has a wavenumber : small means large scales, large small scales.

For a Gaussian random field, which the early universe was to excellent precision, the power spectrum contains all statistical information. It is the Fourier-space twin of the correlation function of lesson L5.2.

Two smaller features are imprinted on top:

  • Baryon suppression: before recombination baryons could not cluster, so a universe with more baryons has less small-scale power.

The S8 tension

Weak gravitational lensing surveys (lesson L5.6) find a few percent lower than predicted from the CMB. Whether this is a statistical fluke, a systematic error, or new physics (for example, massive neutrinos or interacting dark matter) is an active question.

Growth over time

All linear modes grow together, so the whole spectrum simply moves up as : at redshift 2 the power was about 5.7 times lower than today, at redshift 6 about 30 times lower. How P(k) is measured

  • Galaxy redshift surveys (SDSS, 2dFGRS, DESI, Euclid) measure the clustering of galaxies. Galaxies are biased tracers: , with a bias of order 1–3 that must be modelled.
  • Weak lensing measures the matter itself, dark matter included.
  • The Lyman-α forest: absorption by hydrogen clouds in quasar spectra probes small scales at redshifts 2–5.
  • The CMB measures the primordial spectrum on the largest scales.

Together they trace the same ΛCDM spectrum over more than three decades in scale, from thousands of Mpc down to about 1 Mpc.

1. A large value of P(k) at wavenumber k means...

The power spectrum measures the squared amplitude of density waves with wavelength 2π/k.

2. What does the spectral index n_s = 0.965 tell us?

n_s = 1 would be scale-invariant; a value slightly below 1 is a prediction of simple inflation models.

3. Why does the matter power spectrum turn over near k ≈ 0.016 h/Mpc?

The turnover marks the scale entering the horizon at matter–radiation equality; its position depends on Ω_m h.

4. What is σ8?

It is the standard measure of the amplitude of matter clustering; Planck gives about 0.81.

5. Why do galaxy surveys not measure the matter power spectrum directly?

P_gal = b² P with a bias b that must be modelled; weak lensing measures the matter itself.

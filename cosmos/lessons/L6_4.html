L6.4 Reionisation and 21-cm Cosmology

The first stars and galaxies ended the cosmic dark ages and ionised the universe again. The faint 21-cm radio line of hydrogen promises to map this hidden era.

≈ 22 min read · Before this: L4.4, L5.5 · Simulators: 21-cm Global Signal Explorer, Cosmology Calculator, Interactive Cosmic Timeline

In this lesson you will learn

  • What the dark ages and cosmic dawn were
  • How reionisation is measured with quasars and the CMB optical depth
  • The physics of the 21-cm hyperfine line and the spin temperature
  • What 21-cm experiments are trying to detect and why it is difficult

After the CMB: the dark ages

After recombination at 380 000 years (lesson L4.4), the universe was filled with neutral hydrogen and helium gas, slowly cooling as it expanded. There were no stars yet. This period is called the dark ages.

Meanwhile dark matter halos grew hierarchically (lesson L5.5). In the first halos massive enough, around – at redshift 20–30, gas could cool and collapse into the first stars: massive, hot, metal-free Population III stars. Their light marks cosmic dawn.

Reionisation

Ultraviolet photons from the first stars and galaxies had enough energy (above 13.6 eV) to split hydrogen atoms again. Ionised bubbles grew around each galaxy, overlapped, and eventually filled the whole intergalactic medium. This is reionisation: the last major phase change of the universe.

Evidence 1: quasar spectra

Neutral hydrogen absorbs light at the Lyman-α wavelength (121.6 nm). Even a tiny fraction of neutral gas, about one atom in 10 000, absorbs completely. In the spectra of quasars at redshift above about 6, all light blueward of Lyman-α is gone: the Gunn–Peterson trough, first seen in 2001. Evidence 3: galaxies seen by JWST

The James Webb Space Telescope finds many bright galaxies at redshifts 10–14. Whether their ultraviolet light is sufficient, or even more than sufficient, to reionise the universe is an active research question.

A story written in one curve

  1. Dark ages (z ≈ 30–150): the gas cools faster than the CMB, collisions couple to it: a shallow absorption trough.
  2. Cosmic dawn (z ≈ 15–25): Lyman-α from the first stars couples the cold gas again: a deep absorption trough.
  3. Heating (z ≈ 10–15): X-rays from early black holes and stellar remnants heat the gas above the CMB temperature: emission.
  4. Reionisation (z ≈ 6–10): the neutral hydrogen disappears and the signal fades.

The EDGES puzzle

In 2018 the EDGES experiment reported an absorption trough centred at 78 MHz () about twice as deep as standard models allow, which would require new physics. In 2022 the SARAS 3 experiment found no such signal at the claimed depth. The measurement is extremely difficult and remains unresolved.

Why 21-cm cosmology is so hard

Radio emission from our own Galaxy and from other galaxies is 10 000 to 100 000 times brighter than the cosmological signal. Fortunately these foregrounds vary smoothly with frequency while the 21-cm signal does not. Separating them requires exquisite calibration and removal of radio interference from human technology.

Current and future experiments:

  • Global signal: EDGES, SARAS, LEDA, REACH, and proposed lunar far-side antennas.
  • Fluctuations: LOFAR, MWA and HERA have set increasingly tight limits; the Square Kilometre Array (SKA-Low, under construction in Australia) aims to image reionisation directly.

The ultimate prize is the dark ages: in principle 21-cm fluctuations contain far more information about the initial conditions than the CMB, because they probe many more independent volumes.

Remember this

  • After recombination came the dark ages; the first stars formed at z ≈ 20–30.
  • Their UV light reionised the universe, complete by z ≈ 6 according to quasar spectra.
  • The CMB optical depth places the midpoint of reionisation at z ≈ 7.7.
  • The 21-cm hyperfine line of hydrogen at 1420 MHz traces neutral gas; its brightness depends on the spin temperature relative to the CMB.
  • The expected signal shows absorption during the dark ages and cosmic dawn, emission after heating and fading during reionisation; detecting it against bright foregrounds is a major challenge.

1. What ended the cosmic dark ages?

After recombination there were no light sources until the first stars formed at redshift 20–30.

2. What does the Gunn–Peterson trough in quasar spectra show?

Complete absorption blueward of Lyman-α appears in quasars beyond redshift about 6.

3. Planck measures an optical depth τ ≈ 0.054. What does this tell us?

τ integrates the density of free electrons along the line of sight; a quick reionisation at z ≈ 7.7 gives this value.

4. At what frequency would we observe the 21-cm line emitted at redshift z = 9?

ν = 1420.4 MHz / (1 + z) = 1420.4 / 10 ≈ 142 MHz.

5. Why is detecting the cosmological 21-cm signal so difficult?

The tiny signal must be separated from bright, smooth foregrounds and human radio interference.

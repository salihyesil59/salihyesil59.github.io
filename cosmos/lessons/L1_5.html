L1.5 The Discovery of the Cosmic Microwave Background

A mysterious hiss in a radio antenna turned out to be the afterglow of the Big Bang. The story of how the oldest light in the universe was predicted, found and mapped.

≈ 16 min read · Before this: L1.2, L0.3 · Simulators: CMB Sky Viewer, Cosmology Calculator, Spectrum & Redshift Simulator

In this lesson you will learn

  • How the hot Big Bang predicted a relic radiation
  • How Penzias and Wilson discovered it by accident in 1965
  • Why the discovery decided the Big Bang versus steady state debate
  • How COBE, WMAP and Planck turned the CMB into a precision tool

A prediction

If the universe was once hot and dense (lesson L0.5), it must have been filled with intense radiation, like the glow inside a furnace. As the universe expanded, that radiation should have cooled but never disappeared.

In 1948 Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, working with George Gamow on how the Big Bang could make light elements, estimated that this leftover radiation should today have a temperature of about 5 kelvin, just above absolute zero. Such cold radiation would glow at microwave wavelengths.

The prediction was largely forgotten. Radio technology of the time could not easily detect it, and many astronomers were not convinced by the Big Bang model at all.

A clue nobody understood

In 1941 Andrew McKellar found that cyanogen (CN) molecules in interstellar space were slightly excited, as if bathed in radiation at about 2.3 K. Nobody connected this with cosmology until after 1965.

An annoying noise

In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey, wanted to use a large horn-shaped antenna, originally built for early satellite communication, for radio astronomy. To do so, they had to understand every source of noise in their receiver.

They found an unexplained excess signal at a wavelength of 7.35 cm, equivalent to radiation at about 3.5 K. It was:

  • the same in every direction in the sky,
  • the same day and night,
  • the same in every season over the whole year.

They checked everything. They even removed a pair of pigeons nesting in the horn and cleaned out what they politely called "white dielectric material". The noise remained.

Two teams meet

Only about 50 kilometres away, at Princeton University, Robert Dicke, Jim Peebles, Peter Roll and David Wilkinson had independently revived the idea of a relic radiation and were building a detector to search for it. When Penzias heard of their work, he called Dicke. After the call Dicke reportedly told his colleagues: "Boys, we've been scooped."

In 1965 the two groups published companion papers: Penzias and Wilson reported the measurement, and the Princeton group explained it as the cosmic microwave background (CMB). Penzias and Wilson received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The oldest light in the universe

The CMB was released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe became transparent. It has travelled for 13.8 billion years, and its wavelength has been stretched about 1 100 times by cosmic expansion, turning a 3 000 K orange glow into 2.7 K microwaves. The details are in lesson L4.4.

The end of the steady state theory

Why was this discovery so important? The steady state theory said the universe has always looked the same. It had no natural way to produce a smooth sea of radiation with the same temperature everywhere. The hot Big Bang predicted exactly that.

A blackbody fingerprint

A hot, dense early universe must produce a blackbody spectrum, the characteristic shape of light from matter in thermal equilibrium. Measuring the spectrum at many wavelengths would therefore be a decisive test. Ground-based and rocket experiments in the following decades gradually confirmed the shape, but the atmosphere absorbs much of the microwave range, so a satellite was needed.

COBE: a perfect blackbody and the first ripples

NASA's Cosmic Background Explorer (COBE) launched in 1989.

  • Its FIRAS instrument, led by John Mather, showed in 1990 that the CMB is a nearly perfect blackbody with a temperature now measured as K. When the spectrum was shown at a meeting of astronomers, the audience gave it a standing ovation.
  • Its DMR instrument, led by George Smoot, found in 1992 tiny temperature differences of about one part in 100 000 between different directions. These ripples are the seeds from which galaxies and the cosmic web grew (lesson L1.4).

Mather and Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Mapping the ripples

After COBE, experiments mapped the ripples in ever finer detail:

  • BOOMERanG and MAXIMA (balloon experiments, 2000) measured the typical size of the hot and cold spots, about 1°, showing that space is flat (lesson L2.5).
  • WMAP (NASA, 2001–2010) produced full-sky maps and pinned down the age and contents of the universe.
  • Planck (ESA, 2009–2013) made the most detailed maps so far, measuring the basic cosmological parameters to about 1% (lesson L3.4).

The CMB is all around you

There are about 411 CMB photons in every cubic centimetre of space, including the room you are in. In the days of analogue television, a small part of the "snow" on a TV tuned between channels was caused by the CMB.

Remember this

  • The hot Big Bang predicted a leftover radiation of a few kelvin (Alpher and Herman, 1948).
  • Penzias and Wilson discovered it by accident in 1965 as an unexplained, direction-independent noise; the Princeton group explained it.
  • The CMB decided the debate in favour of the Big Bang over the steady state theory.
  • COBE showed a perfect 2.7255 K blackbody and tiny ripples; WMAP and Planck turned those ripples into precise measurements of the universe.

Quiz

Choose an answer to see whether it is right, and why. Score 70% or more and you know this lesson.

1. Who predicted in 1948 that a relic radiation of about 5 K should fill the universe?

Their work on Big Bang nucleosynthesis implied a leftover radiation, though the prediction was largely forgotten until 1965.

2. Which property of Penzias and Wilson's noise showed it came from beyond our galaxy?

A local or galactic source would vary with direction or time. A uniform signal pointed to a cosmic origin.

3. Why was the CMB discovery a problem for the steady state theory?

In the hot Big Bang the CMB is the natural afterglow of a dense early phase; a universe that is always the same has no such phase.

4. What did COBE's FIRAS instrument show in 1990?

The measured spectrum matched a 2.7255 K blackbody extremely precisely, as the hot Big Bang predicts.

5. How large are the temperature ripples first detected by COBE in 1992?

The tiny ripples of about 1 in 100 000 are the seeds of galaxies and the cosmic web.

L4.5 Neutrino Decoupling and the Cosmic Neutrino Background

One second after the Big Bang, neutrinos stopped interacting and have travelled freely ever since. They fill the universe as a second, colder relic background.

Ghost particles

Neutrinos are elementary particles with no electric charge and a tiny mass. They feel only gravity and the weak nuclear force, so they hardly interact with anything: about 60 billion neutrinos from the Sun pass through every square centimetre of your body each second, and almost none of them is stopped. They come in three flavours: electron, muon and tau neutrinos, each with its antiparticle.

In the hot early universe, however, matter was so dense and hot that even neutrinos were constantly created, scattered and absorbed. They were part of the cosmic soup, sharing the same temperature as photons, electrons and positrons.

One second after the Big Bang

The ratio drops below one at about 1–2 MeV ( K), roughly one second after the Big Bang. From then on neutrinos decoupled: they stopped interacting and have streamed freely through the universe ever since. This is the neutrino version of the photon decoupling that produced the CMB 380 000 years later.

The cosmic neutrino background today

The cosmic neutrino background (CνB) is as universal as the CMB:

Photons (CMB) Neutrinos (CνB)
Released 380 000 years 1 second
Temperature today 2.73 K 1.95 K
Number per cm³ 411 336 (112 per flavour, including antineutrinos)

Every cubic centimetre of space contains about 336 relic neutrinos. Yet their energies are so low, about eV, that no experiment has detected them directly. Proposed experiments such as PTOLEMY aim to capture them on tritium, but this remains extremely challenging.

Measuring the neutrinos anyway

Even without direct detection, cosmology "sees" the relic neutrinos through their gravity and their contribution to the expansion rate.

Neutrino masses

Neutrino oscillation experiments proved that neutrinos have mass: the sum of the three masses is at least about 0.06 eV. Today the relic neutrinos are so cold that at least two of the three types move slowly, behaving like a tiny amount of hot dark matter. Their mass slightly suppresses the growth of galaxy clusters and the cosmic web.

Cosmological observations therefore limit the total neutrino mass to roughly 0.1 eV or less, a tighter bound than any laboratory experiment. Pinning down this mass is a major goal of current galaxy surveys.

1. Why did neutrinos decouple from the cosmic plasma about one second after the Big Bang?

The weak rate falls as T⁵ while H falls as T², so their ratio drops below one at about 1 MeV.

2. Why is the cosmic neutrino background colder than the CMB?

The annihilation energy went only into the photons, giving T_ν/T_γ = (4/11)^(1/3) ≈ 0.714.

3. What is the temperature of the cosmic neutrino background today?

0.714 × 2.7255 K ≈ 1.95 K.

4. How many relic neutrinos (all flavours, including antineutrinos) are in each cubic centimetre of space?

About 112 per flavour, 336 in total, compared with 411 CMB photons.

5. How has cosmology detected the effect of the relic neutrinos?

The neutrinos' energy density changes the expansion rate during BBN and leaves imprints in the CMB; both give N_eff ≈ 3.

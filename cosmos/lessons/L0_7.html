L0.7 Orders of Magnitude: Estimating in Your Head Before You Compute

The single most useful skill in physics is knowing roughly what the answer should be. Powers of ten, dimensional analysis, the handful of numbers worth memorising, and how to catch a mistake before it reaches a paper.

In this lesson you will learn

  • Reading and manipulating powers of ten without a calculator
  • Estimating an answer to within a factor of a few, and knowing when that is enough
  • Using units to check a formula before trusting it
  • The cosmological numbers worth carrying in your head
  • Recognising the three ways an order-of-magnitude estimate goes wrong

Why bother, when there is a calculator?

Because the calculator will happily tell you the universe is 13.8 million years old if you typed one wrong exponent, and only you can notice. Every working physicist keeps a rough answer in mind before computing, and compares. Most errors are caught that way, not by checking the algebra.

The skill has a name — an order-of-magnitude estimate, or a Fermi problem, after Enrico Fermi, who estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb by dropping scraps of paper as the blast wave passed and watching how far they blew.

The rule of the game

You are not trying to be right. You are trying to be right to within a factor of a few — one power of ten at worst — using numbers you already know. That is almost always enough to tell a sensible answer from a nonsensical one.

Round in opposite directions

If you round one factor up, round the next one down. Errors that cancel keep you inside your factor of a few; errors that pile up in the same direction do not.

The factor you cannot get

Dimensional analysis gives you the scaling and never the dimensionless number in front. That number is almost always between 0.1 and 10, which is exactly why an order-of-magnitude estimate is allowed to ignore it.

The three ways it goes wrong

  1. A missed exponent. By far the most common. Write every number as and track the exponents separately from the front numbers.
  2. A missing factor that is not of order one. Some numbers really are enormous — the ratio of electromagnetic to gravitational force between two protons is . Estimating is fine; assuming every unknown factor is 1 is not.
  3. Estimating an exponential. during inflation is not "a few". Where a quantity depends exponentially on something, small changes in that something change the answer by powers of ten, and rough is no longer good enough.

When an estimate is not enough

Precision cosmology measures parameters to a percent, and the entire Hubble tension (L6.6) lives in a 9% disagreement. Order-of-magnitude thinking tells you whether a measurement is possible; it can never settle a question like that. Know which kind of question you are asking.

Remember this

  • Estimate first, compute second, and compare. That habit catches most mistakes.
  • A factor of a few is a success; one power of ten is usually still useful.
  • Units check a formula for free and often reconstruct its shape.
  • Memorise a dozen numbers to one significant figure — , a year, a parsec, , , , — and most of cosmology becomes arithmetic.
  • Watch out for exponents, for genuinely huge dimensionless numbers, and for anything exponential.

Quiz

Choose an answer to see whether it is right, and why. Score 70% or more and you know this lesson.

1. What is (3×10⁸) × (2×10⁻⁵)?

Multiply the front numbers, add the exponents: 3×2 = 6 and 8 + (−5) = 3.

2. Roughly how many seconds are there in a year?

365 × 24 × 3600 ≈ 3.15×10⁷ — and π×10⁷ is accurate to half a percent.

3. Which combination of G, M and c has the units of a length?

G is m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², so GM is m³ s⁻², and dividing by c² leaves metres. The Schwarzschild radius is 2GM/c².

The point is to tell a sensible answer from a nonsensical one, not to compete with a calculator.

5. The Hubble time 1/H₀ is about 14 billion years. What is c/H₀ roughly?

c/H₀ = c × (1/H₀) = 14 billion light years ≈ 4 300 Mpc: the same fact written as a distance.

6. Which estimate is order-of-magnitude thinking least able to handle?

When a quantity depends exponentially on something, a small change in that something moves the answer by many powers of ten.

7. Dimensional analysis tells you the shape of a formula. What can it never give you?

That factor needs the real theory — but it is almost always between 0.1 and 10, which is why estimating still works.

8. The critical density is about 10⁻²⁶ kg/m³. How much matter is that?

Dividing by the hydrogen mass 1.7×10⁻²⁷ kg gives about five atoms per cubic metre — emptier than any laboratory vacuum.

"How far?" has many answers

Suppose we see a galaxy at redshift . How far away is it?

  • When the light left, the galaxy was relatively close.
  • While the light travelled, space expanded.
  • Today the galaxy is much farther away than when it emitted the light.

There is no single "distance" in an expanding universe. Instead, cosmologists define several distances, each tied to a specific way of measuring. At small redshift they all agree with Hubble's law, . At large redshift they differ enormously.

Farther galaxies can look bigger

Beyond , a galaxy of fixed size appears larger on the sky the farther away it is. The reason: we see it as it was when the universe was much smaller, so it was physically close to us when it emitted the light we receive. The CMB's last scattering surface, the most distant thing we see, has an angular diameter distance of only about 40 million light-years.

Which distance should I use?

  • Brightness, standard candles, supernovae: luminosity distance.
  • Sizes, angles, standard rulers such as BAO and CMB spots: angular diameter distance.
  • "Where is it now?", counting galaxies in volumes: comoving distance.
  • "How old is the light?": lookback time (or light-travel distance).

Quiz

1. At very small redshift (z ≪ 1), how do the different cosmological distances compare?

The differences between them grow with redshift; nearby they are all approximately cz/H0.

2. Why is a distant supernova dimmer than the simple inverse-square law with its comoving distance predicts?

Redshift reduces photon energy and time dilation reduces the arrival rate, so D_L = (1 + z) D_M.

3. A galaxy at z = 1 has a comoving distance of 11.1 billion light-years in a flat universe. What is its luminosity distance?

D_L = (1 + z) D_C = 2 × 11.1 = 22.2 billion light-years.

4. Why do galaxies beyond z ≈ 1.6 appear larger on the sky the farther away they are?

The angular diameter distance equals the distance at emission, which decreases again for very early times.

5. A news report says a galaxy is '13 billion light-years away'. Which distance does this usually refer to?

It usually means the light has travelled for 13 billion years. The galaxy's comoving distance today is much larger.

L0.6 Olbers' Paradox: Why Is the Night Sky Dark?

A simple question with a profound answer. The darkness of the night sky tells us that the universe cannot be infinitely old, static and uniformly filled with stars.

≈ 15 min read · Before this: L0.1, L0.3 · Simulators: Olbers' Paradox Simulator, Cosmology Calculator

In this lesson you will learn

  • The argument that an infinite, eternal universe should have a bright sky
  • Why interstellar dust does not solve the paradox
  • The real solution, the finite age of the universe and the lifetimes of stars
  • How the expanding universe and the CMB fit into the story

A question a child could ask

Why is the sky dark at night? The obvious answer is "because the Sun is on the other side of the Earth". But think about the stars. If the universe were infinite, unchanging and filled evenly with stars, the night sky should be blazing with light. This contradiction is called Olbers' paradox, after the German astronomer Heinrich Olbers, who discussed it in 1823. Kepler (1610), Halley and de Chéseaux (1744) had already worried about it.

The forest of stars

Imagine standing in a huge forest. Nearby trees are few but look wide; distant trees look thin but there are many of them. If the forest is big enough, every line of sight ends on a tree trunk, and you see a solid wall of wood in every direction.

The same should happen with stars in an infinite universe: every line of sight would eventually end on the surface of a star, and the whole sky would shine as brightly as the surface of the Sun, day and night.

Every shell contributes the same

The distance cancels out. A shell a billion light-years away sends us exactly as much light as a shell of the same thickness nearby. Add up infinitely many shells and the total brightness is infinite. In reality nearer stars would block the farther ones, so the sky would "only" be as bright as a star's surface everywhere.

A failed solution: dust

A tempting answer is that dust between the stars absorbs the distant light. Olbers himself suggested this. It does not work: in an eternal universe the dust would absorb energy until it became as hot as the starlight around it, and then it would glow just as brightly. Absorption only delays the problem.

The real solution: the universe has a finite age

The key assumptions were that the universe is infinitely old and unchanging. Both are false.

  1. Light needs time to travel. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so we can only receive light from a limited region: the observable universe. Light from farther away has not reached us yet.
  2. Stars do not live forever. Stars shine for millions to billions of years and have existed only for the last ~13.5 billion years. There is simply not enough energy in all the stars to fill the universe with light as bright as a stellar surface.

How far would we need to see for lines of sight to hit a star? With about Sun-like stars per cubic megaparsec (a realistic average), a straight line travels about light-years before it meets a stellar surface. The observable universe is some times smaller than that.

A tiny fraction of the sky

Within the 13.8 billion light-years that light has had time to cross, the stars cover only about of the sky. The sky is dark because the universe is too young and too sparsely filled with stars, not because the stars are hidden.

A poet's insight

The first person to state the correct solution was probably the writer Edgar Allan Poe. In his 1848 essay Eureka he suggested that we see darkness in the gaps between stars because light from beyond has not had time to reach us. Lord Kelvin worked out the physics quantitatively in 1901.

What about the expanding universe?

Cosmic expansion also darkens the sky. Light from distant galaxies is redshifted (lesson L0.3), so each photon arrives with less energy, and photons arrive less often. This makes distant sources dimmer than the simple inverse-square law suggests. Detailed calculations show that expansion reduces the night-sky brightness by roughly a factor of two or three, while the finite age of the universe and the finite lifetimes of stars reduce it by an enormous factor. Expansion helps, but it is not the main reason.

The sky is not completely dark

Look at the sky with microwave eyes and it does glow uniformly in every direction. This is the cosmic microwave background, light released when the whole universe was a hot glowing gas at about 3 000 K (lesson L1.5). Back then, every line of sight really did end on a glowing surface, just as Olbers imagined. Expansion has since stretched that light by a factor of about 1 100, cooling it to 2.7 K, far too cold for our eyes to see.

The extragalactic background light

All the galaxies that ever shone also create a faint diffuse glow, the extragalactic background light. Measuring it tells astronomers how much light all stars have produced over cosmic history.

Remember this

  • In an infinite, eternal, static universe full of stars, every line of sight would end on a star and the sky would be bright.
  • Each shell of stars contributes equally, because more stars balance the dimming.
  • Dust cannot solve the paradox: it would heat up and glow.
  • The night sky is dark mainly because the universe has a finite age and stars have finite lifetimes; expansion helps a little.
  • In microwaves the sky does glow: the cosmic microwave background.

1. What does Olbers' paradox claim about an infinite, eternal, static universe filled evenly with stars?

Like trees in an endless forest, stars would cover every direction, making the sky as bright as a star's surface.

2. In the shell argument, why does every shell of stars send us the same amount of light?

The r² from the shell's volume cancels the 1/r² of the inverse-square law, so each shell contributes n L dr.

3. Why can interstellar dust not solve the paradox?

Absorbed energy has to go somewhere. Given enough time the dust reaches the temperature of the radiation and re-emits it.

4. What is the main reason the night sky is dark?

We only receive light from a limited volume, and stars could never have filled the universe with that much light. Expansion helps, but only by a factor of a few.

5. In which part of the spectrum does the whole sky glow almost uniformly?

The cosmic microwave background comes from every direction: light from when the universe really was a hot glowing gas, now cooled to 2.7 K by expansion.

L0.1 Scales of the Universe

A journey from human size to the edge of the observable universe, and the tool that makes such huge numbers manageable.

In this lesson you will learn

  • What cosmology studies and why it needs a special way of writing numbers
  • How to read and use powers of ten (scientific notation)
  • The sizes of planets, stars, galaxies and the observable universe
  • Why looking far away also means looking back in time

What is cosmology?

Cosmology is the science of the universe as a whole: what it contains, how it is arranged, how it began and how it changes over time. Astronomy studies individual objects such as planets, stars and galaxies; cosmology steps back and asks questions about the entire cosmos.

Before we can talk about the expansion of the universe or the Big Bang, we need a feeling for how big things are. Cosmic sizes are so extreme that everyday units and intuition break down. Our first tool is therefore a way of writing numbers.

From a human to the observable universe spans 27 powers of ten. If the Sun were the size of a grain of sand, the nearest star would be another grain about 30 km away, and the Milky Way would be as wide as the orbit of the Moon.

Space is mostly empty

Stars are tiny compared with the distances between them. Galaxies, on the other hand, are relatively close to each other compared with their sizes: Andromeda is only about 25 Milky Way diameters away. This is why galaxies often collide, but stars inside them almost never do.

Hierarchy of cosmic structure

The universe is organised in a hierarchy:

  1. Stars like the Sun, often with planets.
  2. Galaxies, systems of up to hundreds of billions of stars, gas and dust bound together by gravity.
  3. Groups and clusters of galaxies. The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group with Andromeda and dozens of smaller galaxies.
  4. Superclusters and the cosmic web: long filaments of galaxies surrounding huge, nearly empty voids.

On the very largest scales, above a few hundred million light-years, the universe looks roughly the same everywhere. This observation will become a key principle of cosmology in Level 1.

Looking far means looking back

Light travels fast, about 300 000 km every second, but not infinitely fast. Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach us, so we see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago. We see the Andromeda galaxy as it was 2.5 million years ago.

A cosmic time machine

The farther away we look, the further back in time we see. By observing very distant galaxies, astronomers can directly watch the universe as it was billions of years ago. This is one of the most powerful tools in cosmology.

Remember this

  • Cosmology studies the universe as a whole.
  • Powers of ten make enormous and tiny numbers manageable; each step is a factor of ten.
  • Sizes range over 27 powers of ten from a human to the observable universe.
  • Matter is organised from stars to galaxies, clusters and the cosmic web.
  • Because light takes time to travel, looking far away means looking into the past.

Quiz

Choose an answer to see whether it is right, and why. Score 70% or more and you know this lesson.

1. How is the number 3 600 000 written in scientific notation?

Move the decimal point six places to the left: 3 600 000 = 3.6 × 10⁶.

2. How much larger is 10¹⁵ m than 10¹² m?

Dividing powers of ten subtracts exponents: 10¹⁵ / 10¹² = 10³ = 1 000.

3. Roughly how many powers of ten separate the size of a human from the size of the observable universe?

A human is about 10⁰ m and the observable universe about 10²⁷ m across: 27 powers of ten.

4. Why do galaxies collide fairly often while stars inside them almost never do?

Andromeda is only about 25 Milky Way diameters away, but the nearest star is tens of millions of solar diameters away. Stars are like grains of sand kilometres apart.

5. We observe a galaxy whose light has travelled for 2 billion years. What do we see?

Light takes time to travel, so we see the galaxy as it was when the light left it: looking far away means looking back in time.

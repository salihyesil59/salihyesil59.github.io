L5.3 Jeans Instability and Linear Perturbation Growth

Why gravity turns tiny density differences into galaxies, which scales can collapse, and how fast perturbations grow in an expanding universe.

Gravity is unstable

A perfectly smooth universe would stay smooth forever. But if one region is slightly denser than average, it pulls in matter from its surroundings and becomes denser still. Gravity is unstable: small differences grow.

The Jeans criterion

Gravity is not the only force. Pressure resists compression. In 1902 James Jeans compared the two for a static gas cloud.

The Jeans mass before and after recombination

Before recombination, baryons were locked to photons, and the sound speed was about half the speed of light. The Jeans mass of the baryons was around –, larger than the richest galaxy clusters. Ordinary matter could not collapse at all; it oscillated, producing the acoustic peaks of lesson L5.1.

Dark matter got a head start

Dark matter does not interact with photons, so it had no pressure and its perturbations could grow from matter–radiation equality (), long before recombination. When the baryons were released, they fell into the dark matter potential wells that were already much deeper. This is one of the strongest arguments for dark matter (lesson L3.2).

3. How does a density perturbation grow in a matter-dominated expanding universe (linear regime)?

Hubble drag slows gravitational growth from exponential to a power law: δ grows in proportion to the scale factor.

4. What is the Mészáros effect?

When radiation dominated, the expansion was so fast that matter perturbations grew only logarithmically.

5. Why are galaxies hard to explain without dark matter?

Dark matter perturbations had been growing since matter–radiation equality, giving the baryons deep potential wells to fall into.

∑ Cosmology Calculator

Ages, distances and more for any redshift.

Choose a cosmological model and a redshift z. The calculator tells you how old the universe was when the light left the object, how long the light travelled, and the different 'distances' astronomers use.

How to use it

  • Pick a model from Preset (Planck 2018 is today's best estimate) or type your own values.
  • Enter the redshift z of the object you are interested in.
  • Read the results table. Click any row to see what that quantity means.
  • Use the plots to see how each quantity changes with redshift.

Things to try

  • Set z = 1100: this is the cosmic microwave background. How old was the universe?
  • Compare the comoving and light-travel distances at z = 3. Why is one larger?
  • Switch to Einstein–de Sitter. Is the universe older or younger than in Planck 2018?

⤴ Expansion History Explorer

How matter and dark energy shape the past and future.

Adjust how much matter and dark energy the universe contains and watch the scale factor a(t) change. The Ωm–ΩΛ map shows which combinations lead to eternal expansion, a Big Crunch or no Big Bang at all.

How to use it

  • Move the Ωm and ΩΛ sliders, or click anywhere on the Ωm–ΩΛ map.
  • The upper plot shows the size of the universe over time; today is at t = 0 where a = 1.
  • Press Pin curve to keep the current curve for comparison.
  • Read the summary for the universe's age, geometry and fate.

Things to try

  • Find the Big Crunch region: increase Ωm above 1 with ΩΛ = 0.
  • Pin Planck 2018, then set ΩΛ = 0 with Ωm = 0.3. Which universe is older?
  • Cross the dashed 'accelerating' line and watch the curve's shape today.

≋ Spectrum & Redshift Simulator

See spectral lines shift with motion and expansion.

Atoms absorb light at precise wavelengths, leaving dark lines in a spectrum. Move the source or expand the universe and watch the lines slide toward the red (or blue).

How to use it

  • Choose Doppler motion (a moving source) or Cosmic expansion (a distant galaxy).
  • Move the slider. The lower strip shows the observed spectrum; the upper one is the laboratory spectrum.
  • Lines that leave the visible range continue into the infrared (grey area).
  • Try the Mystery galaxy challenge to measure a redshift yourself.

Things to try

  • At what redshift does the red Hα line leave the visible range?
  • Make the source approach you: which way do the lines move?
  • At z = 2, how big was the universe compared with today?

⟋ Hubble Diagram Fitter

Measure the expansion rate from real data.

Plot galaxy velocities against distances and fit a straight line through the origin. Its slope is the Hubble constant H0, and 1/H0 estimates the age of the universe.

How to use it

  • Select a data set: Hubble's original 1929 data or a simulated modern sample.
  • Move the H0 slider until the line follows the points; watch the residuals shrink.
  • Press Find best fit to let least squares find the optimal slope.
  • Compare the Hubble time 1/H0 with the true age of the universe (13.8 Gyr).

Things to try

  • Fit Hubble's data. Why is his H0 about seven times too large?
  • Notice the galaxies with negative velocities in 1929: which ones are they?
  • Fit the modern sample and compute 1/H0 in billions of years.

Used in: L0.5 A History of Cosmology: From Geocentrism to the Big Bang, L1.1 The Cosmic Distance Ladder, L1.2 The Hubble–Lemaître Law

◎ Galaxy Rotation Curve

Uncover dark matter from how galaxies spin.

Stars orbit the centre of a galaxy. If only visible matter pulled on them, distant stars would move slowly. Build a galaxy from a bulge, a disk and a dark matter halo and compare with a measured-style rotation curve.

How to use it

  • Adjust the bulge and disk masses: these are the stars and gas you can see.
  • Turn the dark matter halo on and change its mass.
  • Compare the total curve with the data points.
  • Press Fit halo to data to find the halo mass that matches best.

Things to try

  • Pick a real galaxy from the Galaxy list: 138 measured curves from the SPARC survey.
  • Compare a dwarf (DDO154) with a giant spiral (UGC02885): which needs proportionally more dark matter?
  • Switch off the halo: how does the curve behave in the outskirts?
  • How much dark matter lies inside 30 kpc compared with visible matter?
  • Can you fit the data with a heavier disk and no halo? Why not?
  • Tick Use MOND: can modified gravity explain the curve without dark matter?

◌ Balloon & Raisin-Bread Expansion

Why every galaxy sees all others moving away.

Galaxies sit on a grid that stretches as the universe expands. Pick any galaxy as your home: all others recede from it, faster the farther they are. Light travelling between galaxies is stretched too.

How to use it

  • Press Play to expand the universe; Reset returns to the start.
  • Click a galaxy to stand on it. Arrows show how others move as seen from there.
  • Press Emit light to send a wave and watch its wavelength stretch.
  • Toggle the comoving grid to see that galaxies keep their grid positions.

Things to try

  • Choose a galaxy at the edge. Is the pattern of arrows any different?
  • Compare an arrow twice as long with the distance: is the ratio the same?
  • Emit light, wait until the scale factor doubles, and read the redshift.

△ Curvature Visualizer

Triangles, circles and rulers in curved space.

How to use it

Things to try

⚛ BBN Abundance Explorer

How the first three minutes made hydrogen, helium and lithium.

Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts how much helium, deuterium, helium-3 and lithium formed in the first minutes, depending on the density of ordinary matter. Change the baryon density, add extra neutrino species or change the neutron lifetime, and compare the predictions with the observed abundances and with the CMB.

How to use it

  • Move η₁₀, the number of baryons per ten billion photons. The vertical line marks your value.
  • The coloured horizontal bands are the observed abundances; the green vertical band is the baryon density measured from the CMB.
  • Use Change the physics to add extra relativistic species or change the neutron lifetime.
  • The right-hand panel shows neutrons decaying while the universe waits for deuterium to survive.

Things to try

  • Press From deuterium. Does the result land inside the CMB band?
  • Find the lithium minimum. Can any baryon density fit lithium and deuterium together?
  • Set ΔN_eff = 1. How much does helium change, and could the observations allow it?
  • Lengthen the neutron lifetime to 888 s, the beam-experiment value. What happens to helium?

⁂ 2D N-body Structure Formation

Watch gravity build the cosmic web from tiny ripples.

Tens of thousands of dark matter particles start almost uniformly spread, with tiny ripples. Gravity amplifies the ripples into sheets, filaments and halos that merge into ever larger structures. Compare the growth with linear theory and try warm dark matter.

How to use it

  • Press Play. Time is measured by the growth factor D; D = 1 corresponds to today.
  • Watch the lower plot: at first the density contrast follows linear theory, then gravity takes over.
  • Change the spectral index or choose warm dark matter, then press Apply and restart.
  • A different random seed gives a different universe with the same statistics.

Things to try

  • When does the simulation first deviate from the linear-theory line?
  • Compare n = −2 with n = 0: which one forms large filaments, which one many small clumps?
  • Run the same seed with warm dark matter. What happens to the smallest halos?

⊚ Gravitational Lensing Simulator

Bend light with galaxies, clusters and black holes.

Mass bends the paths of light rays. Put a point mass, a galaxy or a galaxy cluster in front of distant galaxies and see multiple images, arcs and Einstein rings. The Einstein radius uses real cosmological distances, so the ring's size weighs the lens.

How to use it

  • Drag inside the image to move the background galaxy; the orange cross marks its true position.
  • Change the lens and source redshifts and watch the Einstein radius in the measurements.
  • Switch to a field of galaxies to see how a cluster distorts many galaxies at once.

Things to try

  • Tick Switch the lens off to see the sky as it would look without gravity.

⛓ Likelihood & MCMC Explorer

Watch a measurement being made, one step at a time.

Every number in cosmology comes from three pieces: a model, a likelihood that says how well it fits the data, and a way to explore the parameters. Here a random walker explores Ωm and ΩΛ against real supernovae, and the cloud it leaves behind is the measurement.

How to use it

  • Press Run the chain, then Watch it walk to see the walker leave its starting corner and settle into the good region.
  • Read the mean and the ± in What the chain says: that is the measurement.
  • Change the proposal step σ and watch the acceptance rate and the shape of the cloud.
  • Press Run 4 chains to check R̂: four walkers from four corners must agree.

Things to try

  • Set σ = 0.005. The acceptance rate goes above 90% — why is the answer still wrong?
  • Set σ = 0.4. Almost nothing is accepted; what happens to the effective sample size?
  • Tick Assume a flat universe: one parameter instead of two, and a much tighter Ωm.
  • Compare the 1998-like sample with Pantheon+: the same method, twenty-five years of data.

Used in: L7.2 From Data to a Number: Likelihood, χ² and 5σ, L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence, L7.1 Reading a Scientific Plot, L3.3 Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe, L6.6 Current Tensions: the Hubble Tension and the S8 Tension

🪜 Distance Ladder Builder

Climb from parallax to H0 and follow every error bar.

The local Hubble constant rests on three rungs: parallaxes calibrate Cepheids, Cepheids calibrate type Ia supernovae, and distant supernovae measure the expansion. Design the observing programme, watch the uncertainty of each rung flow into H0, and see why a systematic error survives any amount of data. The measurements are simulated.

How to use it

  • Choose an observing programme, or set the numbers of stars and supernovae yourself.
  • Read The three rungs: each panel is one step of the ladder with its own fit.
  • Open Error budget to see how much each rung contributes to the uncertainty of H0.
  • Press Repeat 300× to check the error bar by rerunning the whole measurement.
  • Add a systematic — a parallax zero-point offset or crowding — and compare the shift with the error bar.

Things to try

  • Start from the Key Project preset. Which rung limits it, and what does fixing only that rung achieve?
  • Add 2000 Hubble-flow supernovae. Why does the total error barely move?
  • Set a parallax offset of +20 µas: how many σ does the toy H0 move, and would you notice?
  • Set the true H0 to 67.4. How precise must the ladder be to rule out 73 at 5σ?

Used in: L7.3 MCMC in Practice: Degeneracies, Combined Probes and Convergence, L7.6 Systematics: The Errors That Do Not Shrink with More Data, L1.1 The Cosmic Distance Ladder, L6.6 Current Tensions: the Hubble Tension and the S8 Tension

〰 Standard Siren Explorer

Measure H₀ from a gravitational-wave merger.

A merging pair of neutron stars or black holes gives its own distance away: relativity fixes the amplitude of the wave, so the strain that arrives here is a distance with no calibration behind it. Add the redshift of the host galaxy and you have H₀ without a single rung of the distance ladder — if you can find the host, and if you can tell how the binary was tilted.

How to use it

  • Start from GW170817, the one merger so far whose host galaxy was identified.
  • Watch the chirp: its rising frequency gives the masses, its amplitude the distance.
  • Open Distance vs inclination to see why the distance error is so lopsided.
  • Change the detector network: sensitivity sets the reach, the number of sites sets the sky area.
  • Turn off the host galaxy is known to make it a dark siren and watch the candidates multiply.
  • Raise the number of events until the error crosses the 2% line.

Things to try

  • Reproduce GW170817: 40 Mpc, SNR 32, H₀ to about 15%. Which error dominates?
  • Move the same merger to 400 Mpc. What happens first — the SNR or the host?
  • Compare a face-on and an edge-on binary at the same distance. Which is measured better?
  • How many bright sirens does it take to beat the Hubble tension at 2%?
  • Switch to the Einstein Telescope. How far can a neutron-star merger be heard?

🕸 Redshift Survey Slice

Things to try

🌌 CMB Sky Viewer

The real microwave sky, with the mask and the filters.

This is not a model. It is the WMAP nine-year map of the whole sky: the oldest light there is, released 380 000 years after the Big Bang, with the ripples that every galaxy grew from. Apply the analysis mask the team published, blur the map or keep only its fine detail, and measure what is left — the size of the spots, how alike two points are, and whether the distribution is the Gaussian inflation predicts.

How to use it

  • Turn the KQ85 mask off and on. The bright band is our own galaxy, not the early universe.
  • Raise Smooth by and watch the rms fall in the summary.
  • Tick keep only what is smaller to subtract the blur and leave the degree-scale spots.
  • Open Correlation function and read the spot size off the half-way point.
  • Switch the projection between Mollweide and longitude–latitude.

Things to try

  • With the mask off, how much larger are the extremes? Whose light is that?
  • Smooth by 5°. How much of the 67 µK is left, and what has been thrown away?
  • In How much survives blurring, find the scale where the curve bends.
  • Compare the histogram with the Gaussian. Inflation predicts that shape.
  • Find the coldest spot. It is a real feature, and it has its own literature.

⚛ Recombination Explorer

Why the universe became transparent at 3000 K, not 158 000 K.

380 000 years after the Big Bang the free electrons were captured by protons, and light could suddenly travel for ever: that light is the cosmic microwave background. Follow the number of free electrons as the universe cools, compare the naive equilibrium answer with the real one, and see where the CMB photons scattered for the last time.

How to use it

  • Read Free electrons: the solid curve is the real history, the dashed one the Saha equilibrium it lags behind.
  • Open The last-scattering surface to see where the photons we receive today were last deflected, and how thick that shell is.
  • Open Why so cold? to count the photons that can still ionise an atom.
  • Change the baryon density or today's CMB temperature and watch the moment of transparency move.

Things to try

  • At what temperature is half of the hydrogen neutral? Compare it with 13.6 eV.
  • How much later than Saha does the real recombination happen, and why?
  • Switch to the logarithmic axis. How many electrons never find a proton?
  • Double today's CMB temperature. Does last scattering happen at a different temperature, or only at a different redshift?
  • Lower Ωb h² to 0.01. What happens to the electrons left over?

⬤ Halo Mass Function Explorer

How many haloes of each mass, when — and why clusters weigh σ8.

Every galaxy lives in a halo of dark matter, and the halo mass function says how many haloes of each mass there are. It follows from the ripples in the early universe alone: a region collapses once its overdensity passes a threshold. Go back in time to the first star-forming haloes, or count the giant clusters on today's sky and see how steeply they depend on σ8.

How to use it

  • Move σ8 and Ωm; the dashed curves stay at Planck 2018 for comparison.
  • Mass function shows how many haloes there are of each mass at the chosen redshift.
  • Clusters on the sky counts every halo above the threshold out to z = 1, for several values of σ8.
  • Rare peaks shows σ(M): a halo forms where it crosses δc = 1.686.
  • Across cosmic time follows small haloes, galaxies and clusters from z = 20 to today.

Things to try

  • Raise σ8 by 10%. How much do the clusters above 10¹⁵ M☉/h change?
  • Lower the threshold to 10¹³ M☉/h and repeat. Is the effect smaller?
  • Go to z = 20. How many haloes can make the first stars?
  • Find the redshift at which the typical collapsing halo was the size of the Milky Way.
  • Switch between Press–Schechter and Sheth–Tormen. Where do they disagree most?

📡 21-cm Global Signal Explorer

Listen to hydrogen from the dark ages and the first stars.

Neutral hydrogen absorbs and emits at 21 cm, and that line, stretched by the expansion, arrives today at radio frequencies between about 10 and 200 MHz. Averaged over the whole sky it records the history of the gas: cooling in the dark ages, the first starlight, the first X-ray heating and reionisation. Change the astrophysics and compare with the contested EDGES detection.

How to use it

  • The global signal shows the brightness temperature against frequency; the top axis gives the redshift.
  • Move Lyman-α coupling to change when the first stars light up, and X-ray heating to change when and how much the gas is warmed.
  • Three temperatures shows why: absorption wherever the spin temperature is below the background.
  • What couples the spins shows collisions giving way to starlight.
  • Add an extra radio background to try to reach the depth EDGES reported.

Things to try

  • Where is the dark-ages trough, and why can it not be seen from the ground?
  • Switch the X-ray heating off. What happens to the emission?
  • Make the first stars form later. Which way does the trough move in frequency?
  • How big a radio background does it take to match EDGES?
  • Delay reionisation to z = 6. What changes at the high-frequency end?

⛏ Dark Matter Detection

Build an underground detector and draw your own exclusion curve.

If dark matter is made of WIMPs, a few of them should bounce off atomic nuclei in a detector deep underground. Choose the target, the size, the threshold and the background of your detector, pick a WIMP, and see the recoil spectrum it would leave, the events of a single run, the yearly modulation — and the exclusion curve a null result would draw.

How to use it

  • Start from an experiment, or change the target, exposure, threshold and background yourself.
  • Set the WIMP mass and cross-section. The summary says how many events it would give and whether it would be discovered, excluded or hidden.
  • Exclusion curve: everything above your curve would have been seen. Your WIMP is the round dot.
  • A simulated run draws one random outcome; press Run the experiment again for another.
  • Annual modulation shows the few-per-cent yearly swing as the Earth orbits the Sun.

Things to try

  • With the xenon detector, at what WIMP mass is the limit strongest? Why does it weaken on both sides?
  • Set the WIMP mass to 5 GeV. Which detector can still see it?
  • Double the exposure with no background. How much lower does the curve go? Now add 20 background events.
  • Find a WIMP that is excluded but would not have been discovered.
  • Run the same experiment several times. How much does the number of events jump?
